Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) replaces or augments hardcoded
reward heuristics with a preference-learned reward model.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE THREE CORE STAGES OF RLHF IN WAREHOUSE ROBOT COORDINATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. What Feedback People Would Provide:
   - Rather than assigning explicit scalar numbers to every transition, human evaluators
     are shown pairs of completed trajectory rollouts: Trajectory Sigma_1 vs. Trajectory Sigma_2.
   - Evaluators express a qualitative comparative preference:
         Sigma_1 >~ Sigma_2  (or Sigma_2 >~ Sigma_1, or equal preference).
   - Humans prefer trajectories that:
     * Avoid uncomfortably close proximity or rapid near-misses near the doorway;
     * Eliminate wasteful idling or excessive stalling once a path is clear;
     * Demonstrate natural courtesy (e.g., smoothly yielding in an alcove rather
       than erratic back-and-forth jitter).
   - This produces a dataset D = {(Sigma_1^(i), Sigma_2^(i), y^(i))} of pairwise comparisons.

2. What a Learned Reward Model Would Predict:
   - A parameterized neural network R_psi(s, a) (or R_psi(tau)) is trained on the
     pairwise preference dataset using the Bradley-Terry preference model:
         P(Sigma_1 > Sigma_2) = sigma( sum_{t} R_psi(s_1,t, a_1,t) - sum_{t} R_psi(s_2,t, a_2,t) ),
     where sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z}) is the sigmoid function.
   - The reward model learns a latent scoring function that assigns higher cumulative
     scalar values to trajectories exhibiting smooth, socially efficient coordination
     and penalizes subtle inefficiencies (like hesitant waiting or aggressive posturing)
     without requiring an engineer to hand-tune numerical penalty coefficients.

3. How an RL Agent Would Use That Reward Model:
   - In the subsequent policy optimization phase (e.g., using PPO or DQN), the agent
     interacts with the warehouse environment.
   - Instead of receiving a hand-engineered reward r = -0.1 - b + 5k, the agent
     queries the frozen reward model:
         r_t = R_psi(s_t, a_t).
   - The RL algorithm uses these predicted rewards to compute TD errors, update its
     Q-values, and steer its policy toward behaviors that maximize human-preferred
     trajectory attributes.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. HAND-WRITTEN PENALTY EDITING VS. PREFERENCE REWARD MODELING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question:
Does editing the hand-written collision penalty train a reward model? Explain why this
is fundamentally different from learning a reward from preferences.

Answer:
NO, manually editing the hand-written collision penalty does NOT train a reward model:

1. Hand-Tuning is Manual Rule Engineering:
   - Modifying a scalar parameter (e.g., changing collision penalty from -10 to -50)
     is manual heuristic tweaking by an engineer.
   - No data is collected, no loss function is minimized, no statistical model is
     parameterized, and no preference generalization takes place.
   - It relies entirely on the developer's intuition and often leads to unintended
     pathologies (such as the robot choosing to freeze forever to completely eliminate
     any risk of incurring the severe penalty).

2. Learning from Preferences is Supervised Reward Modeling:
   - Training a reward model in RLHF is a machine learning process:
     * It fits a function approximator R_psi to empirical behavioral judgments from
       human annotators.
     * It captures implicit, high-dimensional trade-offs that are virtually impossible
       to write down in a simple piecewise formula (e.g., balancing transit speed,
       safe following distance, and minimal disturbance).
     * The reward model generalizes to unseen states and trajectories based on learned
       features rather than fixed hardcoded thresholds.
