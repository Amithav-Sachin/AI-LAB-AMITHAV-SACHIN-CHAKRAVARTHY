1. PRE-IMPLEMENTATION CONTROLLER DESIGN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
(a) Explain why observing only A's position is insufficient:
    Answer:
    If the controller observed only Robot A's position, the environment would become
    partially observable (a POMDP). Robot B moves simultaneously, enters the narrow
    corridor, and occupies cells. Without observing B's location, the state transition
    p(s'_A | s_A, a_A) would be non-Markovian and unpredictable—moving Right into (2,2)
    could result in a valid passage or a catastrophic collision depending entirely
    on hidden information. A centralized joint state (p_A, p_B) restores full
    observability and the Markov property.

(b) Write down how you will represent both positions:
    Answer:
    Each robot's position is represented by a length-25 one-hot vector with a 1.0 at
    index (5*r + c). The joint state is formed by concatenating the two vectors:
        joint_input = [one_hot(p_A), one_hot(p_B)]  in R^50.

(c) Calculate the number of joint actions:
    Answer:
    Each robot has 5 individual actions: {Up, Down, Left, Right, Wait}.
    The joint action space is the Cartesian product:
        |A_joint| = 5 x 5 = 25 joint actions.
    Encoding: j = 5 * a_A + a_B, where j in {0, 1, ..., 24}.
    Decoding: a_A, a_B = divmod(j, 5).

(d) Draw one possible collision-free route for both robots:
    Answer:
    Because the doorway at (2, 2) is exactly 1 cell wide, both robots cannot pass
    at the same time. One robot must yield by stepping into an open side cell.
    A valid collision-free schedule:
    - Step 0: A at (2,0), B at (2,4)
    - Step 1: A moves Up to (1,0); B moves Left to (2,3)
    - Step 2: A moves Wait at (1,0); B moves Left to (2,2) [Doorway]
    - Step 3: A moves Wait at (1,0); B moves Left to (2,1)
    - Step 4: A moves Down to (2,0); B moves Left to (2,0) [Wait: B can move to (2,0) while A steps aside to (1,1)]
    Alternatively, A moves to (1,1) (sidestepping into the upper alcove) while B
    passes straight through (2,2) -> (2,1) -> (2,0). Once B arrives, A drops back
    to (2,1) and traverses (2,2) -> (2,3) -> (2,4).

(e) Explain where one robot might need to wait or move aside:
    Answer:
    The narrow doorway at (2, 2) connects the left room (columns 0-1) and the right
    room (columns 3-4). The obstacle wall spans columns c=2 at rows {0, 1, 3, 4}.
    Because the passage has width 1, counter-directional robots will collide if both
    enter column 2 simultaneously. Therefore, one robot must either wait in its room
    or yield by stepping vertically (e.g., into row 1 or row 3) before entering the
    doorway, allowing the other robot to clear the passage.

4. ANALYSIS OF THE EMERGENCE OF YIELDING & REFLECTION QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(a) Which robot entered the passage first?
    Answer:
    Robot B entered the passage first. At Step 3, Robot B moved Left from (2, 3)
    into the doorway cell (2, 2), while Robot A was holding at (1, 1).

(b) Where did the other robot yield?
    Answer:
    Robot A yielded by moving out of the central corridor at Step 2, taking action
    'Up' from (2, 1) into cell (1, 1) and subsequently (0, 1) in the upper room.
    This cleared the line of sight and allowed Robot B unhindered passage.

(c) Is avoiding collisions sufficient to count as success?
    Answer:
    NO. Avoiding collisions is a necessary condition for success, but not sufficient.
    The primary objective is the delivery task—both robots must reach their respective
    goals. A policy that chooses to wait indefinitely safely avoids all collisions
    (receiving 0 collision penalties), but fails the warehouse delivery mandate and
    times out. This illustrates the classic "freezing robot" dilemma in multi-agent RL.

(d) How would the joint-action output size change for three robots?
    Answer:
    With centralized joint action selection, the output space grows exponentially
    with the number of agents (curse of dimensionality):
        |A_joint| = |A|^N = 5^N.
    - For N = 1 robot:  5^1 = 5 actions.
    - For N = 2 robots: 5^2 = 25 actions.
    - For N = 3 robots: 5^3 = 125 linear outputs.
    - For N = 4 robots: 5^4 = 625 linear outputs.
