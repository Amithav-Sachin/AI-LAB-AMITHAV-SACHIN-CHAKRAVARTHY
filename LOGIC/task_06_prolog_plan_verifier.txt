3. ANSWERS TO CONCEPTUAL QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(a) Why does Prolog return true for can_move(a,b)?
    Answer:
    When Prolog evaluates the goal `?- can_move(a, b).`, it unifies the goal with
    the head of the rule:
        can_move(X, Y) :- connected(X, Y).
    with variable substitutions {X = a, Y = b}.
    This generates the sub-goal `connected(a, b)`.
    Prolog searches its knowledge base for this atom and encounters the ground
    fact `connected(a, b).` on line 1.
    Since the body of the rule is satisfied by an identical ground fact in the
    knowledge base, the resolution proof succeeds and Prolog returns `true`.

(b) Why does it not establish can_move(a,c)?
    Answer:
    When Prolog evaluates `?- can_move(a, c).`, it unifies with the rule, producing
    the sub-goal `connected(a, c)`.
    Prolog then searches the knowledge base for `connected(a, c)`.
    The database only contains:
        connected(a, b).
        connected(b, a).
        connected(b, c).
        connected(c, b).
    None of these facts unify with `connected(a, c)`.
    Under Prolog's Negation as Failure (NAF) and the Closed World Assumption (CWA),
    any proposition that cannot be proved through finite resolution from the
    knowledge base is considered false.
    Because there is no direct corridor between waypoint A and waypoint C, the query
    fails and Prolog returns `false`.

(c) What is the relationship between the Prolog rule can_move(X,Y) and the
    logical implication Connected(X, Y) -> CanMove(X, Y)?
    Answer:
    The Prolog rule:
        can_move(X, Y) :- connected(X, Y).
    is the Horn clause representation of the first-order predicate logic implication:
        forall X, Y. (Connected(X, Y) -> CanMove(X, Y))
    
    In classical propositional/first-order logic, an implication (P -> Q) is
    logically equivalent to (~P v Q).
    In clausal normal form (Horn clause), this is written with at most one positive
    literal:
        CanMove(X, Y) v ~Connected(X, Y)
    
    In Prolog syntax, the head of the clause (the positive conclusion CanMove) is
    written on the left, and the body (the antecedent condition Connected) is
    written on the right, separated by `:-` (meaning "if"):
        Head :- Body.  <===>  Body -> Head.
    
    During inference, Prolog uses backward chaining (SLD resolution) to work
    backwards from conclusion to premise, applying Modus Ponens in reverse to
    determine whether the antecedent holds.
